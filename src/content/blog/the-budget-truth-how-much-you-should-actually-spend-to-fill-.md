He spent half his day chasing people who had already booked with someone else or, worse, people who weren't even in his service area. He was paying for "leads," but his calendar had holes in it and his crew was sitting around on Tuesdays.

When Mike sat down with us, he asked one simple question: *"I'm tired of being lied to. What should I actually be spending to keep my bays full without getting ripped off?"*

If you're running a plumbing company in Raleigh or an HVAC outfit in Lewisville, you're asking the same thing. You don't care about "brand awareness" or "impressions." You care about the phone ringing and your crew having a full schedule of profitable jobs.

Here is the straight-talk guide on what to spend on marketing in 2026 to actually move the needle.

### The "Golden Rule" of Marketing Math

First, let's clear the air. Marketing isn't a "cost"—it's an investment. If you spend $1,000 and it brings in $5,000 in booked jobs, you didn't "lose" a thousand bucks; you bought $4,000 in profit.

But you can't just throw money at the wall and hope it sticks. To avoid the "HomeAdvisor Trap"—where you pay for junk leads that don't convert—you need a budget based on your actual revenue.

For the vast majority of local service businesses, the benchmarks for 2026 are these:

**1. The Growth Phase (Sub-$1M Annual Revenue)**
If your shop is doing under a million a year, you should be allocating **5% to 10% of your gross revenue** toward marketing. 
*   *The Math:* If you're doing $600k a year, that's $3,000 to $5,000 a month. 
*   *The Goal:* At this stage, you aren't trying to "dominate the state." You're trying to make sure that every single person in your zip code and the next two over knows your name the second they need a pipe fixed or a car protected.

**2. The Scaling Phase ($1M to $3M Annual Revenue)**
Once you've hit that million-dollar mark, you have more overhead and likely a larger crew to keep busy. Your spend should shift to **8% to 12% of gross revenue**.
*   *The Math:* At $2M in revenue, you're looking at $13,000 to $20,000 a month.
*   *The Goal:* You're no longer just "getting leads." You're optimizing for the *best* leads. You're spending more to filter out the tire-kickers and the "can you do it cheaper?" crowd, focusing instead on the high-ticket jobs that make your crew the most money per hour.

### Where the Money Actually Goes (And Where It Doesn't)

This is where most agency "experts" lose you. They show you a colorful chart of "traffic" increasing. You look at that chart, then you look at your phone, and it hasn't rung in three hours. 

Traffic is a vanity metric. **Booked jobs are the only metric that matters.**

If you are spending your budget on the following, you are likely wasting your money:
*   **Shared Leads:** If you are paying a company that sells the same lead to five other contractors, you aren't buying a customer; you're buying a race to the bottom on price.
*   **"Brand Awareness" Packages:** If an agency tells you that you need "more eyeballs" on your Facebook page but can't tell you how many calls that will generate, fire them.
*   **Generic SEO "Maintenance":** Paying $1,000 a month just to "keep your rankings" without a direct tie to the phone ringing is a tax on your business.

**So, where should your money actually go in 2026?**

#### 1. Owning Your Own Lead Pipeline
Instead of renting leads from a third party, spend your money building a system you actually own. This means a Google Business Profile that is optimized to convert and a website that doesn't just look "pretty" but is designed to make the phone ring. When you own the lead, you aren't competing with four other guys in a frantic text-message war. You are the only option the customer is talking to.

#### 2. High-Intent Search (The "I Need This Now" Money)
When a pipe bursts in a basement in Cary, NC, that homeowner isn't scrolling Instagram. They are going to Google and typing "emergency plumber near me." 
Investing in search—specifically through Google Ads and local map optimization—puts you in front of the customer at the exact moment they have a problem. This is the fastest way to get jobs on the calendar.

#### 3. The "Dream Client" Filter
Not all jobs are created equal. A window tint shop doesn't make the same profit on a basic sedan as they do on a full PPF wrap for a brand-new Porsche. A portion of your budget should be spent on "filtering." This means creating specific offers and landing pages that attract the high-ticket clients and repel the people looking for the cheapest quote in town.

### The "Bank Account Test" for Your Marketing

If you're not sure if your current spend is working, stop looking at the reports your agency sends you. They are designed to make the agency look good, not to make you rich.

Instead, use the **Bank Account Test**.

Every Monday morning, ask these three questions:
1.  **How many new calls came in last week?**
2.  **How many of those turned into booked jobs?**
3.  **What was the total job value of those bookings?**

If your agency can't answer those three questions with a simple spreadsheet, they aren't marketing your business—they're just spending your money. 

If you spend $3,000 a month and it results in 20 new booked jobs with an average ticket of $500, you've generated $10,000 in revenue. That's a win. If you spend $3,000 and "traffic is up 40%" but you only booked two jobs, you're being robbed.

### How to Scale Your Spend Without Risking the Shop

A common fear for owners is: *"If I increase my spend from 5% to 10%, will I just be wasting more money?"*

The answer is: not if you scale based on capacity.

The biggest mistake we see is the "on-off switch" approach. An owner gets a few slow weeks, panics, and cranks the marketing budget to the max. Then, the phone starts ringing off the hook, they get overwhelmed, and they shut the marketing off entirely.

This kills your momentum. 

The goal is to find your "Saturation Point." This is the amount of marketing spend that keeps your crew working at 90% capacity without you having to work 100 hours a week to manage it. 

Once you hit that point, you don't stop spending. You shift your budget. Instead of spending to get *more* jobs, you spend to get *better* jobs. You raise your prices, you target wealthier neighborhoods, and you focus on the most profitable services in your catalog.
